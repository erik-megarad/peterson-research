# Electing the maximum around a one-way ring

Peterson's basic algorithm elects the process that originally owned the largest identifier. Candidates move between processes, so the process carrying the largest candidate is not automatically the winner. The checked result combines announcement safety, conditional eventual election, and a bound on actual messages.

Each active process sends its candidate, receives its left neighbor's candidate, sends the larger of those two, then receives another candidate. It keeps the received neighbor candidate if that candidate is at least as large as both competitors. Otherwise it becomes a relay. A process announces when an incoming identifier equals its immutable original identifier. Relays receive into their current candidate register and forward that value. This account follows the publication's `>=` comparison and printed `receive(tid)`. A retained manuscript uses strict `>`; equivalence with that version is not claimed.

The formal model has any positive finite ring size, injective identifiers in a linearly ordered type, all processes initialized, and unbounded reliable FIFO edges. Sends, deliveries and local instructions are separate transitions. There are no faults or changing participants. The one-process ring has a self-loop. Reliability and local fairness are explicit premises of eventual election; safety and the finite message bound need no fairness. FIFO is a chosen formal interpretation supporting the source's neighbor argument, rather than an explicit queue definition quoted from the paper.

The proof first tracks identifier origin and preserves a live maximum in control locations where it can still affect the protocol. An auxiliary sequence of candidate rounds then explains which message occurrence may reach each process. A refinement argument connects that bookkeeping to the actual asynchronous queues, including partially completed rounds. It rules out a smaller owner receiving its own identifier as a winning announcement. Fair delivery and local execution move the surviving maximum back to its owner.

For message counting, each completed candidate round removes at least half the competitors. The proof charges active and relay sends on real edges, including the final single-candidate traversal. The bound is `3*n*(1+floor(log2 n))`, through first announcement, for every finite prefix at that boundary, including unfair ones. Delivery and receive do not count as additional transmissions. Ending the accounting at first announcement does not give the other processes a global stop instruction.

The sharper constant in the paper, optimized Fibonacci phases, winner notification, network shutdown, delay, average-case/lower bounds, dynamic startup, faults, non-FIFO channels and executable implementations remain outside this result. CSLib supplies the labelled transition-system interface; Mathlib supplies finite-set, permutation, sum and logarithm facts. The occurrence and frontier arguments remain specific to this protocol.
